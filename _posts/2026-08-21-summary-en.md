## [Researcher accidentally logs military call routing data via abandoned ENUM delegation](https://lina.sh/blog/hijacking-e164-arpa) ⭐️ 8.6/10

A security researcher discovered a live but apparently abandoned e164.arpa/ENUM DNS delegation and, by querying it, accidentally logged hundreds of thousands of phone call routing records, including for military bases. The incident was documented in a blog post that has sparked substantial discussion on Hacker News. This highlights serious security and privacy risks in the telephony/DNS infrastructure, showing how abandoned or misconfigured delegations can expose sensitive routing data. It also demonstrates real-world impact on military communications and raises questions about how such gaps persist and who is responsible. The author accidentally collected call routing records rather than call content, and the data appears to have come from a private or internal ENUM-style service that was mistakenly exposed in the public e164.arpa zone. The blog post also notes that the issue was reported but the researcher was not rewarded, and commenters add that similar private ENUM services are still in use over VPNs.

hackernews · gavide · Aug 21, 13:11 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49387570)

**Background**: ENUM (E.164 Number Mapping, RFC 6116) is a protocol that uses the Domain Name System (DNS) to map traditional telephone numbers (E.164 format) to Internet addressing schemes such as SIP URIs. The e164.arpa zone was created as the DNS root zone for storing these mappings, intended to unify the public switched telephone network with internet telephony. Although the idea never became truly public, some private implementations remain active, and misconfigurations can expose sensitive routing information.

## [New Analysis Examines How Speech Recognition Benchmarks Are Gamed](https://huggingface.co/blog/asr-benchmark-optimization) ⭐️ 8.2/10

The blog post, published on Hugging Face, provides a technical analysis of how speech recognition benchmarks can be optimized or gamed, coining the term 'benchmaxxing' in this context. It argues that broader measurement alone is insufficient to address the problem. This matters because benchmark leaderboards heavily influence model adoption and research priorities; if ASR benchmarks can be easily gamed, reported accuracy may not reflect real-world performance. The analysis offers a framework for researchers and practitioners to critically evaluate speech recognition models rather than relying solely on scores. The post addresses a problem—benchmark optimization—that has been widely discussed in other ML fields but has proven difficult to quantify in speech recognition. It likely examines common optimization strategies and evaluates detection metrics, while acknowledging that broader measurement alone does not solve the issue.

rss · Hugging Face Blog · Aug 21, 00:00

**Background**: Benchmarks are standardized datasets used to evaluate and compare machine learning models; optimizing for a benchmark means deliberately tuning a model to perform well on a specific test set rather than improving general capability. In speech recognition, benchmarks like LibriSpeech are common, but performance varies dramatically across different audio conditions, making it easier for models to overfit to benchmark-specific traits. Test set contamination—where benchmark data leaks into training data—is a well-known form of this optimization in LLMs, and similar concerns apply to ASR, though measurement is more challenging.

This matters because as coding agents become more common, teams need reliable ways to measure productivity, and Willison's argument challenges the long-standing principle from The Mythical Man-Month about conceptual integrity versus speed. His points about cognitive capacity and the Winchester Mystery House problem highlight new risks and trade-offs of AI-generated code.
