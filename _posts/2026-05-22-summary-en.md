## [Wealth vs Income Tax Conversion Explained](https://paulgraham.com/winc.html) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Paul Graham's essay presents a mathematical framework to convert between wealth and income tax rates by dividing by the return on capital, using a 5% risk-free rate to equate a 1% wealth tax to a 20% income tax. This conversion highlights how seemingly small wealth taxes can be equivalent to large income tax increases, potentially altering public debate on tax policy and wealth inequality. The conversion assumes wealth generates income at the risk-free rate, which may not hold for non-productive assets or when the wealthy borrow instead of selling assets to avoid income tax.

hackernews · bifftastic · May 22, 15:43 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48237422)

**Background**: Wealth tax is levied on an individual's net worth (assets minus liabilities), while income tax is levied on earnings from labor or capital. The conversion factor (e.g., 20) depends on the assumed rate of return on capital.

**Discussion**: Commenters criticize the essay for oversimplifying: they argue that for most people, wealth is not generating income at the risk-free rate, and the wealthy often avoid income tax by borrowing. They also note that the equivalence fails for those with little or no wealth, making a wealth tax more progressive in practice.

This marks the first time a general-purpose AI has solved a major open problem in mathematics, demonstrating advanced reasoning capabilities. It could transform how mathematical research is conducted, making breakthroughs more accessible and accelerating discovery. The problem, posed by Paul Erdős in 1946, asks for the maximum number of unit-distance pairs among n points in the plane. GPT-next disproved the conjecture for under $1000 in compute cost, suggesting efficient AI-driven research is feasible.

rss · Latent Space · May 21, 07:28

**Background**: The Erdős planar unit distance problem is a central question in discrete geometry. It asks: given n points in the plane, what is the maximum possible number of pairs that are exactly one unit apart? Erdős conjectured a lower bound of n^{1 + c / log log n}. Despite decades of effort, the problem remained open until this AI-driven disproof.
